# $100M Money Models (Alex Hormozi) — Practitioner Reference

## Quick reference

**Core thesis:** Get rich by making more profit from each customer than it costs to acquire and service them — within 30 days. A Money Model is a deliberate sequence of offers (what you offer, when, and how) that maximizes how many customers you get, how much they pay, and how fast they pay it. A $100M Money Model makes enough 30-day profit from ONE customer to acquire and service at least TWO more, removing cash as the constraint on scale.

**Trilogy positioning:** $100M Offers = what to sell (an offer so good people feel stupid saying no). $100M Leads = how to find buyers (advertise). $100M Money Models = how to get them to buy (a sequence of offers).

## Core concepts

### Money Model — definitions
- A Money Model is a sequence of offers in a specific order: find every opportunity to solve a customer's problem and offer to solve it, at the moment they realize they have it.
- Full form: a deliberate sequence of offers — what you offer, when you offer it, and how you offer it — to make as much money as possible as fast as possible.
- **Good Money Model (minimum bar):** more profit from a customer than it costs to get AND service them within the first 30 days.
- **$100M Money Model:** more profit from ONE customer than it costs to get and service MANY customers within 30 days — cash stops limiting scale. Target: one customer's 30-day profit funds acquiring and servicing at least two more.

### The 30-Day Rule
Cover the full cost of acquiring a customer within 30 days. Mechanical reason: any business can get 30 days of interest-free money on a credit card. Acquire the customer on credit, recoup inside the billing cycle, pay the balance, redeploy the same credit line. Repeat indefinitely. This is why "30 days" recurs through every framework.

### Compounding growth math
Make customers 2x as valuable AND get 2x as many AND collect 2x as fast → 8x growth (2×2×2). Triple all three → 27x. This is the rationale for stacking all four offer types rather than optimizing one.

### The bad-money-model death spiral (what you're avoiding)
Spend on ads → spend exceeds earnings → cut advertising → fewer customers → cut ads entirely → float business on personal cash/loans/credit → sell equity to keep lights on → wait months/years to recover → fall behind → lose it all. Root cause: profit drips in slower than acquisition cost goes out.

### The Four Offer Types
1. **Attraction Offers** — turn strangers into customers.
2. **Upsell Offers** — get people to spend more cash.
3. **Downsell Offers** — get people to say yes when they would have said no.
4. **Continuity Offers** — keep people buying.

Logic chain: no attraction offer → few customers; only one offer → little cash; add upsells → cash, but many say no; downsells convert the nos; continuity locks in recurring cash. Each works standalone, in any order or combination; the most profitable businesses use all four.

### Operating rules (apply to every play)
- Every business has a Money Model. Replace "this won't work for my business" with "how will I make this work for my business?"
- Design your own model; don't copy — some models fit some businesses better.
- If a customer asks for money back, give it back — entitled or not ("don't take blood money"). Fix goofs.
- Hard selling is for weak products. Make offers available when the customer has the problem; don't convince against will. It's a numbers game.
- Obey the law, especially around "free," sweepstakes, and advertising claims. Consult counsel.
- Be transparent. If facts aren't compelling, change reality or reframe — never lie. A bad reputation can't be bankrupted away.

## Attraction Offers

Purpose: generate leads and convert them to customers with something free or discounted, while collecting enough up-front cash to cover acquisition + delivery cost multiple times over — funding the next customer immediately.

Key framing: "free," "discount," and "$1" are interchangeable points on one continuum (free = 100% discount). Strangers can't verify value but fully understand price, so discounts make anything a great deal — the greater the discount, the better. People search for one thing and buy another; attraction offers make that happen on purpose.

### 1. Win Your Money Back

**Definition:** "If you do X within Y time within Z rules, you get it free." Customer pays up front; hitting the goal/criteria returns their money as cash or store credit.

**Three qualification structures:**
1. **Results** — hit the outcome (make $X/mo, lose Z lbs) by any path. They bet on their ability to reach the goal.
2. **Actions** — do the behaviors (attend all sessions, log progress, submit photos, homework) regardless of results. They bet on following directions.
3. **Actions + Results** — both. Best for people who lack the skill to succeed alone; you prescribe the path.

**Criteria design — three requirements:**
1. Easy to track — ideally automatic (phone steps, word counts, dated photos).
2. Actually gets results — copy what your best customers do; if criteria "look too easy," they're probably realistic.
3. Advertises the business — build posting, tagging, referrals, reviews, and testimonials into the terms.

**Why it makes money:** NOT from failures. (a) Many won't qualify even with realistic conditions; (b) winners stay as customers — but only if an upsell is ready to absorb their winnings. "We don't get customers to make a sale, we make sales to get customers."

**Key numbers and decision rules:**
- ~10% of all customers will ask for money back (data from thousands of gyms). Budget for it.
- Only run this offer if baseline refund rate is below 5%; otherwise fix the product first.
- Store credit converted the same number of customers as cash-back in testing → offer store credit. To still advertise "free," pair with an unconditional satisfaction guarantee (adding it didn't materially raise refund requests).
- Claimed >$1B in industry-wide sales from this offer.

**Store-credit application formula (critical, links to Rollover Upsell):** apply winnings to something MORE expensive, spread over time — never free months up front. Example: $200/mo product, $600 winnings → NOT 3 free months. Instead $600 ÷ 12 = $50/mo discount → they pay $150/mo for 12 months. People who pay nothing fall off; skin in the game keeps engagement.

**Meetings-as-offers:** make required check-ins part of the criteria; each is an upsell slot. Gym template: (1) Nutrition Orientation (before photos → supplement offer), (2) Progress Check-in (membership offer), (3) Transformation Feedback (after photos → membership offer again; if already bought, offer prepay-a-year discount).

**Two retention plays:**
- "Make Everyone A Winner": ~halfway through, privately credit the program toward the next offer whether or not they hit the goal — lowers failure anxiety, lifts retention.
- "Let the Losers Win": at program end, for those who failed and refused the first upsell, frame finishing as the real win and credit their deposit toward staying long-term.

**Fit:** anything people start and quit — weight loss, fitness, skills, business-building, beauty, self-care, time management, mental health. Works on new, current, and previous customers.

**Templates:** B2C "28-Day Blueprint" (deposit back if: attend all calls, weekly group post, daily journaling, attend feedback + transformation sessions). B2B "5 Customers in 5 Days" (deposit back if: send 100 messages/day, report stats, attend daily training, post homework, attend day-5 call). Physical product "1,000,000-Mile Car" (a real offer): buy a new car, drive it 1,000,000 miles, turn it in, take pictures + appear in a press release → full original purchase price credited toward the next car. This variant extends WYMB to one-time physical goods: the "money back" becomes a rollover credit on a more expensive follow-on purchase, tied to an extreme usage/loyalty milestone plus publicity terms.

### 2. Giveaways

**Definition:** advertise a chance to win a big Grand Prize in exchange for contact info + eligibility answers + qualifying actions. One person wins publicly; everyone else is contacted privately with a discounted "runner-up" offer on the same thing (the "partial scholarship").

**Six-step procedure:**
1. **Pick a Grand Prize** — the thing you want everyone to buy. Assign a monetary value as price anchor (sell $5,000 of value at $2,000 → advertise the $5,000).
2. **Pick the promotional offer** — the core offer enhanced with a discount/bonus or minorly changed from the Grand Prize to ethically justify the lower price, anchored against the Grand Prize value. Name it anything: scholarship, gift card, voucher, store credit.
3. **Collect contact info + permission to follow up.** Add eligibility questions — fit checks ("Do you own a vet clinic?") plus the ammo bank: Why should we pick you? Why this program? Why now? Why does this matter to you? Why is this important to you? What's your goal? Answers let you make the promotional offer's value specific to each lead on the claim call, which gets more of them to buy the core offer. Add qualifying actions (attend a call, post, join a group) — raises engagement and promotes the giveaway. Trade-off: more entry friction → fewer but better-qualified entrants.
4. **Deadline: 3–7 days** from promotion start. Update entrants daily on all channels — countdown + value + social proof. Treat it like a mini launch (whisper–tease–shout).
5. **Announce the winner publicly.**
6. **Contact everyone else privately:** they "qualified for something else" — book a call same/next day. Claim window: 7 days, with a second countdown. Usage deadline once claimed: hours, up to 5 days max.

**Numbers and rules:**
- **Discount sizing: promotional discount = 10–30% of gross margins.** Worked example: Grand Prize valued $5,000, retail $2,000; everyone else pays $1,800 (10% off retail) — framed against the $5,000 value, a 10% discount reads as a 64% cost-to-value difference.
- Urgency in three places: to ENTER (3–7 days), to CLAIM (7 days), to USE (hours to 5 days).
- Scarcity: cap by time, entries, or both; cap entries at how many people you can personally contact within 7 days.
- **Two-prize referral mechanic:** a second grand prize goes to whoever referred the winner → unlimited referral entries, ~2x leads, and referrers police quality.
- Flop diagnosis: the Grand Prize wasn't grand enough (event tickets flopped; a $50,000 equipment bundle + a year of core product crushed).
- Downsell provision: open claim calls with "you qualified for two prizes"; present the promotional offer first; if refused, apply the same percentage discount to any other suitable product.
- Recurring variant: spread the discount over the longest period they'll agree to; auto-bill at normal rates after.
- Legal: someone must actually win; rules must state prize + qualifications + that multiple people can win prizes; consult counsel (illegal-lottery risk).

**Template ladders:** dentist — $6,000 braces prize / $2,000 gift card promo. Services — $5,000 one-year package prize / $2,000 voucher toward a 1-year agreement. Consulting — $12,000 16-week turnaround prize / $6,000 partial scholarship.

### 3. Decoy Offer

**Definition:** advertise something free/cheap (the decoy); when leads engage, present it side-by-side with a far more valuable premium offer. Either purchase wins: premium = profit now; decoy = customer to upgrade later. "Close everyone."

**Origin logic ("turkey talk"):** know what gets customers results better than they do — the free/cheap thing physically can't deliver the result (a tan takes 5–10 spaced sessions; a $5 5-day pass can't), so the premium offer becomes the obvious solution. "Give customers what they want now, so you can give them what they need later."

**Proof numbers:** free option (1 session/week, no support, no guarantee) vs $399 "Ultimate" (unlimited sessions, 1-1 coaching, personalization, results-or-repeat-free guarantee) → 8 of 10 took the $399 option.

**Construction rules:**
- Build the decoy by stripping components, using older versions, removing personalization and ALL guarantees. It only needs to get leads to engage.
- Make the contrast huge: decoy as basic as reasonable, premium as awesome as possible. Not making money fast → increase the contrast.
- Advertise benefits/dream outcome, not features. Product detail belongs in the sales presentation.
- **Free vs discount rule:** free offers → more leads, lower show rates; discount offers → fewer leads, higher show rates. Use discounts where no-shows are costly (doctors, lawyers, dentists).
- Present premium first when possible. If they ask for the decoy: permission question — "Are you here for free stuff or lasting results?" Most say results → skip to premium. If "free stuff," present both, then ask "which do you think will get you to your goal faster?"
- Assumed close: talk as if buying is a formality — bored regularity, "let me get your ID and credit card."
- Optional Surprise Benefit: gift decoy-takers a few zero-cost premium features to build goodwill and prime the upsell.

**Four equivalent ways to state one discount (test which converts):** Percentage Off (25% off) = Absolute Amount ($300 off) = Free Portion (3 months free) = Total Package ("one year for $900 ($1,200 value)"). Identical math; conversion differs by market.

### 4. Buy X Get Y Free

**Definition:** customer buys something and gets other stuff free. Converts a discount (weak) into a free offer (strong) — everyone understands "free"; few know a fair price.

**Core reframing math:**
- 3 shirts at $10 each ($30) ≡ 1 shirt at $30 + 2 free. Same price, more "free."
- With real discount: 3 shirts at $6.67 ($20, 33% off) ≡ 1 shirt for $20 + 2 free.
- Boot Factory: $200/pair boots → "Buy one pair for $600, get two pairs free" — tripled the unit price, same $600 total, free-driven demand.
- Service duration (all $1,800 for 18 months): Buy 12 Get 6 Free (good) < Buy 9 Get 9 Free (better) < Buy 6 Get 12 Free (best) — the most free stuff wins.

**Rules:**
- **Permanently raise prices BEFORE giving stuff away** to preserve margins. Don't fake it — actually raise them; some will pay the raised a-la-carte price.
- More free than paid: "buy one get two" beats "buy two get one."
- Free items can differ from paid items (buy $10 shirt, get $20 of socks free); more cheap free items can beat fewer expensive ones — test.
- Cash discipline: if you collect a year of payments in a month, budget delivery for the full term. Selling what you can't deliver is illegal and reputation-ruining.
- **Fast-cash play for existing customers:** offer bulk prepay (buy 10 months get 2 free) to current recurring customers, capped at 10% of the customer base to protect recurring cash flow.
- Prepaid customers keep buying — keep making offers; their wallets "refresh."
- One-time-purchase businesses (tourists): make the single purchase as large as possible.
- Retention side-effect: buy-2-get-2-free keeps a 3-month-average customer 4 months → more upsell windows.

### 5. Pay Less Now or Pay More Later

**Definition:** two options. (A) Pay Later — $0 today, card on file, billed full price after a deadline; cancellable only if they met the conditional satisfaction guarantee's conditions. (B) Pay Now — 20–50% discount plus extra bonuses, charged immediately. Advertisable as "free"; offer Pay Now after they accept Pay Later — the card is already on file, so converting is frictionless.

**Design rules:**
- **Promise a clear yes/no measurable result** deliverable within the window (e.g., rate pain 1–10 before/after). Vague promises → cancellations.
- **Conditional satisfaction guarantee:** cancellation allowed only if they met participation conditions (attendance, appointments, data submitted) — set conditions to whatever extracts maximum value from the product.
- **Physical products:** the conditional guarantee is a return condition — the customer avoids the pay-later charge only by returning the item in like-new condition before the billing deadline (14-day clothing trial: $0 now then $149 in 14 days, or $97 now + accessory).
- Give extra attention to anyone claiming they didn't receive what was promised before the cancellation deadline — those are the cancellations you can still save.
- **Balancing loop:** too many choosing Pay Later → deepen the Pay Now discount/bonuses; too many Pay Now → the reverse.
- **10% threshold:** if >10% of Pay Later customers cancel, you overpromised, set guarantee conditions too low, or priced too high. Some cancellation is a normal cost of business.
- Recurring version: higher ongoing rate starting in 30 days, or pay less today and lock the lower rate for good + bonuses.
- **Seed the upsell early:** mention the next product exists and sells out ("everyone asks about X — we'll get there"), then return to delivering — implies everyone buys, lifts conversion.
- **Pairing rule:** use this as the downsell for physical products and one-time services; use Trial With Penalty for recurring products/services.

**Template:** real estate 3-day workshop — Later: $0 then $500 unless canceled; Now: $299 + recordings + 1-1 expert call + materials; upsell: $30,000 six-month first-deal program.

## Upsell Offers

**Definition:** an upsell is whatever you offer next. Purpose: maximize 30-day profit from customers you already have. Current customers buy at far higher rates than strangers.

**Problem–solution cycle (core mechanism):** every offer that solves a problem reveals the next problem; the upsell sells the solution to the problem your last offer revealed — so every offer, including upsells, opens the door to another.

**Burger economics (why upsells make or break the model):** $2.00 burger → $0.25 profit. + Fries → +$0.75. + "Make it a meal" (drink) → +$1.75, total $2.00 profit (8x). + Supersize (+$1) → $3.00 profit (11.6x). The thing you sell most is rarely where profit lives. Find your "Do you want fries with that?"

**Why upsells fail (diagnose by these three):** wrong thing (doesn't solve their problem / too different), wrong time (before they've experienced the problem), wrong way (they don't believe you) — or combinations.

**Three upsell directions:** more of the same (quantity), better version (quality), new/complementary (different).

### 1. Classic Upsell

**Definition:** offer the solution to the customer's next problem the moment they become aware of it — "You can't have X without Y" (car→insurance/gas; bike→helmet→lights; exercise course→nutrition course).

**Tactics checklist:**
- **Actually have a second offer.** One product = "you barely have a business — you have a front end." Businesses have 5x'd by simply adding an upsell.
- Offer the higher-profit upsell first.
- **"Say No to Say Yes":** "You don't want anything else, do you?" — the trained "no" reflex completes the sale.
- **Surprise and Delight:** hold 4 closing bonuses; add one at a time to fence-sitters; if they buy early, give all four anyway.
- **Hyper Buying Cycle:** new life events (weddings, babies, new hobbies, moving) open a short window of concentrated spending — keep making offers during it.
- **Bonuses create upsell problems on purpose:** free earmuffs with coat storage → $30 to store the earmuffs.
- **Speed raises perceived value:** put the thing in their hands before they say yes — harder to give back than to refuse.
- **Bundle + name:** bundling turns nine asks into one; name bundles by customer type/result ("Fastest Results," "Transformation Package," "Minimum Package"); later peel items out as Feature Downsells.
- **Integrate upsells into delivery:** embed the next product inside the first (meal plans listing optional supplements) so customers ask for it.
- **BAMFAM — Book A Meeting From A Meeting:** never end an appointment without scheduling the next one and its reason. A customer should know when and why they'll next see you before they leave.
- Upsell as many times as there are problems you can solve; the worst outcome is not asking when they'd have said yes.
- **Charge for guarantees/warranties/insurance:** add 5–50% to price instead of including protection free. Art studio: +10% for damage-replacement coverage → 30% of customers buy it, pure profit on something formerly free.

**Small–Medium–Large price-nudge model:**
- Push Large: Small $5 / Medium $8 (deliberately above the "rational" $7) / Large $9 → mediums migrate to large.
- Push Medium: Small $6 (above rational $5) / Medium $7 / Large $9 → smalls migrate to medium.
- Meta-rule: many smalls → bump to medium; many mediums → bump to large; many larges → raise all prices.

### 2. Menu Upsell

**Definition:** for businesses with multiple products/options. Four stacked tactics, in this order:
1. **Unsell** — cross out what they DON'T need ("You're not pregnant, so we cross out lymphatic massage"). Builds trust, spotlights what they do need. Stock products partly to cross them out; encourage employees to help customers "game the system."
2. **Prescription Upsell** — detailed, personalized usage instructions presented as if they already own it ("take two of these after lunch"), integrated with what they already bought. Detailed + personalized beats vague + general; removes the buy/don't-buy question.
3. **A/B Upsell** — ask preference, not permission: "chocolate or vanilla?" Either answer = a sale. Anything can be A/B'd: quantity, start dates, payment method, flavors, time slots, media, delivery speed, sizes, colors, materials, personnel, communication channel.
4. **Card On File** — "Wanna just use the card on file?" Removes hidden buying costs. First-purchase variant: "What card do you wanna use?"

**Supporting rules:**
- Nudge inexperienced buyers: "this is my favorite," "X is usually a safe bet." Nudge hardest on what you want to move.
- Sold out? Sell anyway: take payment, order it, set delivery expectations — no inventory required.
- **The 'Economist' Play (sell both of two options):** Digital $59/yr vs Digital+Print $125/yr → everyone bought digital. Fix: Digital $59 / Print $125 / Digital+Print $125 → customers flock to the $125 bundle. Rule: present A, B, and C(=A+B) with C priced equal to B.

### 3. Anchor Upsell

**Definition:** present the premium (most expensive) offer FIRST; when the customer balks, "rescue" them with the main offer, which now looks cheap. Mirror image of the Decoy Offer.

**Five steps:**
1. Present the Anchor — the premium thing at **5–10x the main offer's price**.
2. Get "The Gasp" — expect a mini panic. The bigger the gasp, the more they buy.
3. Come to the rescue — ask if they care about the premium differentiator ("Do you care about the designer much?"). Have the fallback pre-staged before the reaction.
4. Present the main offer — relief; it now reads as a great deal.
5. Ask how they want to pay — "Which card do you prefer?"

No gasp? They find the premium reasonable — close the premium.

**Construction rules:**
- Main and premium offers must share the same PRIMARY features (a suit is a suit); differ only on SECONDARY features (designer, materials, insurance term, early access). Same primary features at ~1/5 the price = obvious deal.
- Don't treat the anchor as fake — sell it sincerely as something you'd be happy to deliver; going through the motions kills the effect (a friend tripled profits after making the premium real). Only move on after a pause, hesitation, or request.
- Two bonus effects: anchored customers spend more than planned; some actually buy the premium (outsized profit from a handful of sales).
- **Asymmetric-loss rule:** worse than a $1,000 offer to a $100 budget is a $100 offer to a $1,000 budget — the first loses $100, the second loses $900. ALWAYS have premium offers ready even if few buy.

**Template ladders:** lawn care — $1,000/wk premium (owner's cell, fancy mulch, natural pest control) vs $200/wk main. Newsletter — $199/mo (all back issues + 24h early) vs $19/mo (new issues only).

### 4. Rollover Upsell

**Definition:** credit some or all of a customer's previous purchases toward the next, more expensive offer. The "missing link" that converts Win Your Money Back winners into long-term customers instead of churners.

**Three design decisions — WHO / WHAT / HOW:**
- **WHO (4 situations):** (1) re-engage lapsed customers (6+ months gone); (2) rescue your own upset customers instead of refunding; (3) "rescue" competitors' upset customers by crediting what they paid/owe elsewhere; (4) upsell regular current customers.
- **WHAT:** more / better / new-different — but always something MORE expensive than the credit.
- **HOW:** full or partial credit; applied up front or spread over time.

**Pricing formula:** next offer ≥ 4x the rollover credit → maximum 25% effective discount, preserving profit. Bigger discounts = less profit per sale but more sales (laws of discounting).

**Rules and plays:**
- Urgency: one-time, decide-now, "once-in-a-customer-lifetime" offer; they can still pay full price later.
- Rollover BEFORE refunding an unhappy customer — roll into a do-over or a different product.
- Competitor poach script: "I saw your negative review… I'll credit whatever payments you have left with them toward ours." Lead source: scrape contacts from competitors' negative reviews, or create a complaint venue for your industry.
- **Winback case:** 200 personalized videos to past customers offering $4,000 credit → ~20% took it → ~$1,900,000 extra annual revenue from one day of recording.
- **Gift Card Play:** sell gift cards at 90%+ off ($200 card for $20), limit 2 per customer, usable only on OTHER people → buyers gift them (referral engine; ask who to make it out to + for an intro). Card face value = 20% of the next offer's price ($200 card rolls into a ≥$1,000 offer). Unused cards = pocketed revenue; customers pay you to refer friends.

## Downsell Offers

**Definition:** any offer made after a "no." Two levers: change HOW THEY PAY (pay-now vs pay-over-time) or WHAT THEY GET (quantity, quality, or a different thing).

**Cardinal rule:** never the same thing for less. Dropping an identical product's price (the $5,000→$400 insurance anti-pattern) destroys trust in every price you quote. You may offer something DIFFERENT for less.

**The Rules of Downselling:**
1. They said no to THIS offer, not all offers — make another offer.
2. Downsells are trades: if you give something, get something.
3. Personalize, don't pressure: more of what they like, less of what they don't, price to match. Serving alternatives is a service.
4. Offer the same things in new ways — 100 offers for one product, not 100 products.
5. Never drop price just to close — that's discounting, not downselling. Payment plans change when they pay, not what they pay.
6. Customers talk about price. Test prices deliberately (specific price, specific cohort, planned ahead) — never improvised mid-sale.

**"Business terrorists" rule:** people demanding the same thing cheaper — don't negotiate. Pay less NOW → payment plan. Pay less OVERALL → feature downsell. Pay less "just because" → never.

### 1. Payment Plan Downsell

**Definition:** same product, same total price; some cash up front, remainder scheduled. Insight: "it costs too much" usually means "too much UP FRONT" — payment plans get discount-level conversion at full price.

**Seven-step ladder (stop when they buy; up to nine total offers in one conversation):**
1. **Reward paying in full rather than punish paying over time:** present the price WITH interest built in, then offer prepay as a discount — "It's $15… but $10 if you prepay. That's what most people do." Same math as charging interest; friendlier frame + price anchor.
2. **3rd-party financing → credit card → layaway.** Financing: the finance company pays you today. Credit-card reframe: "Would you rather I decide your payment terms or you decide?" → they choose → suggest their credit card (you're paid today; they finance themselves). Layaway: any installments, any reasonable timeline, product delivered only after full payment — most flexible for them, lowest risk for you.
3. **Half now, half later:** "When's the next time you get paid?" → half down today, half on payday. If refused: "What's the most you can put down today?" then balance on payday. Anchor to paychecks — biweekly beats monthly for 30-day profit.
4. **Temperature check:** "On a scale from 1–10, how bad do you wanna do this?" 8+ → keep payment-planning ("we're gonna figure out a way"). ≤7 → ask "Why not a 10?" and pivot to Feature Downsells.
5. **Three payments:** 1/3 down + 1/3 on each of the next two paychecks (or months).
6. **Evenly spread payments** across the delivery term (16-week program = 16 weekly charges).
7. **Free Trial** — the Trial With Penalty. Sale pauses here.

**Seesaw variant (for junior salespeople):** "Giant monthly payments or tiny ones?" → tiny → "It normally costs $XXX; prepay today = huge discount and zero monthly payments." If unaffordable: adjust the down payment until the monthly rate fits (bigger down = lower monthly). If still no: temperature-check, then physically move to their side of the table and walk the options together.

**Operating tactics and data:**
- **Built-in upsell:** mid-plan, periodically re-offer the original paid-in-full discount on the remaining balance — customers forget it exists; some jump. Pay reps the same bonus for closing balances. "If you give people the option to pay slower, they will" — incentivize faster.
- **Declined-payment recovery:** schedule charges on paydays; if declined, re-run several times the SAME day (deposits land at different hours) — recovers about a third of declines.
- **Health metric:** after adding plans, total close rate should rise while the NUMBER of paid-in-fulls stays constant (10 leads → 3 PIF; with downsells → 3 PIF + 3 plans = 6). If PIF count drops, you're leaking full payers into plans.
- **Profitwell churn data (14,000 subscription businesses), billing cadence vs monthly churn:** monthly billing → 10.7%; quarterly → 5%; annual → 2%. Therefore always present most-cash-up-front first — it maximizes both 30-day cash AND retention.

### 2. Trial With Penalty

**Definition:** free trial where the customer pays ONLY if they fail to meet your terms. Mirror image of Win Your Money Back (pay then earn it back vs free unless non-compliant). Terms = the same actions/results that create excellent customers — swipe them from your WYMB criteria. Use as the LAST downsell, after payment plans fail. Best for recurring products/services where the customer must do work to get results.

**Lift math:** 3/10 buy the main offer; downsell 4 more onto the trial; post-trial upsell converts 3 of the 4 → 6 total sales — doubled customers from the same leads.

**Five-element sales procedure:**
1. Offer the trial LAST: "How about we just get ya started for free… Let me get your ID and we can get the process started — fair enough?"
2. **Always get a credit card:** "What card do you wanna use?" If they balk: "That's just how we've always done it." Still refuse → walk them out. No card + no commitment to the work = don't sell them (they complain more, convert less).
3. **Sell staying and paying:** "If this program got you the result, will you stay long-term?" Must be yes or no trial. Integrity frame: you won't hit long-term goals during a trial; you'll have to stay on after.
4. **Explain fees AFTER taking the card** ("bet on yourself" frame: fees keep you on track; comply and it's all free). Fees before card = more resistance. Have customers initial each fee clause (forces reps to explain them).
5. **Make check-ins required** — each is a named-benefit meeting and an upsell slot; charge for missing them "because it's the only way we can get you results."

**Fee structure:** prefer many small fees over one lump ($500 product with 10 requirements → $50 per miss, not $500 on first miss) — unless one miss genuinely wrecks results. Let people make up a missed criterion once before billing; miss the make-up and billing is justified.

**Post-trial playbook (three outcomes):**
- **Liked it** → billing already automatic; still meet and upsell longer-term/higher tier.
- **Hated it** → absorb all blame, get angrier at yourself than they are, ask what should've been different, position the higher-level offering as the better fit — ~half buy.
- **Didn't use it** → multiple outreaches; offer to waive fees if they meet; restart or refit. Don't bill non-starters — a small fee isn't worth a 1-star review.

**Tuning:** nobody takes the trial → lower requirements/penalties. They take it but don't comply → better fee-benefit explanation + mandatory meetings. They don't stay after → sell staying/paying harder, deliver better, align front-end with back-end.

**Naming:** just call it a "Free Trial." If asked why it works this way: "This is just how we've always done it" / "People get the best results this way."

**Card-capture variant:** $1 first month instead of free — justifies taking the card and validates it for future auto-billing.

### 3. Feature Downsell

**Definition:** lower the price by changing WHAT they get — lesser quantity, lower quality, cheaper alternatives, or removing components. Formula: take something away, lower the price, ask "how about now?"

**Flagship case:** high-ticket seller with one full-price option closed 25/100. Downsell = remove the money-back guarantee for a lower price → 35/100 now buy the MAIN offer (the guarantee's value becomes visible once removed) + 40 take the downsell = 75% close, tripled.

**Value mechanics:** every feature has a price and a value; removal cuts both. Remove stuff they HATE + big price cut → they take the downsell. Remove stuff they LOVE + small price cut → they re-choose the original (they "re-upsell themselves"). Therefore **remove features from highest to lowest value** so customers self-select into the most expensive option that fits.

**Standardized process:** first cut something VALUABLE with a small price drop (forces reconsideration of the original); then keep removing features and lowering price until they buy — better they get something than nothing. Pre-script the feature combinations once you learn customer preferences.

**Feature inventory (levers to dial down for downsells or up for premium tiers):**
- Quantity: units, sessions, duration (3-month supply → 1 month; 4 sessions → 2).
- Product quality: older versions, cheaper/lower-status materials.
- Service quality: time availability (set times vs anytime); days (M/W/F vs all); hours (9–5 vs 24h); session length (15 vs 60 min); location (one vs all); cancellation (fees vs free); response speed (minutes/hours/days); delivery speed (line vs priority, same-day vs next week); service ratio (1-on-1 vs one-to-many vs many-to-one); channel (text/chat/video); provider seniority (owner vs veteran vs new hire); live vs recorded; in-person vs remote; DIY vs DWY vs DFY; expirations (forever vs limited); personalization (generic vs bespoke); guarantee length (1 year vs life), coverage (specific vs any), terms (unconditional vs conditional).
- Remove whole features (drop calls, keep chat+email). Final rung: **DFY → DIY product swap** (chiropractor → home tools; painter → paint + daily sprayer lease; workshop instead of done-for-you).

**Process rules and plays:**
- Name tiers aspirationally ("Whale Package," "Total Transformation" — airline First/Business/Economy pattern). Name the cheapest **"The Minimum"** — implies a floor and enables the close: "so nothing more than the minimum package then?" (no-that-means-yes).
- **Temperature check after two failed downsells:** 1–10 scale. 8+ → switch to Payment Plan downselling ("we're gonna figure out a way"). ≤7 → "What would a 10 look like?" and recombine features to match. **Alternating feature and payment-plan downsells makes you very difficult to refuse.**
- Micro-closes: after each downsell ask "Deal?" or "Fair enough?" — few people watch you adjust the offer for them and then call it unfair.
- Stay a helpful guide (collaborative deal-finding) — you can then downsell indefinitely without exhausting the customer; pushiness exhausts fast.
- **Free-orientation rescue:** after all DFY refusals — "come to a free orientation on X tomorrow"; ~half show; nearly all bought the DIY product solving the same problem.
- **Downsell current customers before they cancel:** unused features → proactively offer a lower package priced for what they use; they recommit or stay cheaper. Downsold-to-fit customers have the **second-highest lifetime value** of all customers.
- **Barter downsell:** "$100 off if you (1) review us on all sites, (2) video testimonial, (3) public posts at start/middle/end, (4) introduce two friends. Deal?" — the advertising is worth more than the discount to you; the discount is worth more than the effort to them.

Closing reframe for the section: "Everybody buys something." Not 100 products with one offer — 100 offers for the same product.

## Continuity Offers

**Definition:** ongoing value exchanged for ongoing payments until cancellation. "You sell once, but get paid again and again."

**The continuity math:** $1,000 one-time to 100 people → 10 buy → $10,000 now, $0 later. Same thing at $50/mo → 40 of 100 buy; kept 20 months = $1,000/customer → $2,000 now + $40,000 over time, plus 4x the customer base to upsell. Con: continuity as a standalone attraction offer starves 30-day cash — hard to advertise profitably.

**Placement doctrine:** make continuity LAST. Run cash-rich Attraction → Upsell → Downsell first; then the continuity offer; then upsell prepaid bulk months at a discount to recover 30-day profit; auto-roll into continuity after the bulk period. No successful continuity business he's seen runs a standalone membership offer — all attract with trials/bonuses, then upsell prepaid terms.

**Fit test:** ongoing value → ongoing payments. A one-day workshop paid forever is silly (that's a payment plan); ongoing service for one price forever is a mistake.

Three offers, built from three ingredients: **bonuses (add value), discounts (cut cost), fees (penalize leaving).** Prefer rewards over punishment; Waived Fee is the exception when a traditional contract fits.

### 1. Continuity Bonus

**Definition:** give an awesome bonus for signing up today; the bonus is typically worth MORE than the first continuity payment. **Advertise the bonus, not the membership.**

**Proof case (gym):** old flow — 34/100 join a six-week challenge, ~17 convert to membership weeks later. New flow — pitch the challenge, then "want it free?": free if they become members + member-exclusive bonuses, then immediately upsell a discounted 6-month prepay. Result: 15/100 still take the challenge (up-front cash), 40/100 go straight to continuity, ~8 of the 40 take the prepay. Membership sales roughly tripled.

**Anchored-bonus close (script sequence):**
1. Sell the benefits of the bonus first — not the membership.
2. Anchor with its high value; let it shock.
3. "Do you want to know how you can get this for free?"
4. "Become a VIP member today and you'll get it all as a free gift. Or buy it standalone for $XXX — which would you prefer?"
5. Stack: "On top of that… amazing thing 1, 2, 3," citing individual dollar values.

**Design rules:**
- Bonus types mirror upsells: more-of-same (2 years of back newsletters), complementary (free nutrition with fitness membership), upgrade (free gold tier with bronze, limited time).
- Keep bonuses related to the core offer or you attract the wrong customers.
- Use things you already have/do (archives; onboarding you'd deliver anyway — price it, then gift it).
- Cross-media bonuses: physical bonus on digital products and vice versa — often cuts CAC by more than the bonus costs.
- Realistic anchor pricing only — previously-sold products make believable anchors; fake values destroy trust.
- Title bonuses: status names (silver/gold/diamond) awarded at 3/6/12 months; customers can value the title above material bonuses.
- Free bonus ↔ steep discount are interchangeable framings ($200 membership + free $1,000 program ≡ $1,000 program for $1 with a $200 membership).
- Gate bonuses behind membership to force continuity; or sell bonuses standalone for up-front cash.

**Standalone-vs-continuity pricing table (his tested data; $199/mo membership reference):**
| Standalone price (multiple of first month) | % choosing continuity |
|---|---|
| 1.33x ($399) | 50% |
| 1.66x ($499) | 60% |
| 2x ($599) | 70% |
| 2.33x ($699) | 80% |
| 2.66x ($799) | 90% |

Principle over exact numbers: the pricier the standalone relative to continuity, the more choose continuity. Corollary: people pay ~33% more to avoid continuity — half will buy a one-time option priced 33% above the recurring one (more up-front cash per head).

**Bulk prepay math:** "buy five months get one free" — if just 1 in 8 members takes it, 30-day profits rise 50%. Laws of discounting apply.

**Commitment trade:** bonuses only for 3/6/12+ month commitments → fewer sign-ups, more committed ones. This is the trade.

### 2. Continuity Discount

**Definition:** free time (product/service) in exchange for a longer commitment — Buy X Get Y Free done continuity-style. Origin: one year of free trash service for a five-year paid contract → locked up commercial accounts, brutal year one, flood of cash after.

**Four placements for the discount:**
1. **Up front** — free period first, official term starts after. Only where contracts reliably get enforced (cell, storage, real estate, equipment, collateral). Skip if churn is high; delays cash.
2. **At the end** — on-time payment of every installment EARNS the bonus time at the end.
3. **Spread over the term** — 3 free months on a 12-month commit at $200/mo = $600 → $50/mo off all 12 months; optionally keep the discount for life if all payments were on time.
4. **After the first 1–2 payments** — collect first cash (covers ads + delivery), then apply the discount; frame as "first and last month," "last month up front," or an activation fee. Also validates the payment method.

Trade-off: front-loaded discounts convert more but churn more; back-loaded convert fewer but churn less; spread keeps cash flowing at the full discount.

**Revenue micro-plays (exact numbers):**
- **Bill in 4-week cycles, not monthly** (self-described highest value-per-word note in the book): a year has 13 four-week cycles vs 12 months = 8.3% more revenue at identical perceived price ("$100 every 4 weeks" converts the same as "$100/month"). At 20% margins, +8.3% revenue ≈ +41% annual profit. Zero extra work.
- **Extend, don't eat, the term:** "3 months free with a year" = pay 12 get 15 (extend), not pay 9 of 12. Keep the shorter version as a Feature Downsell.
- **+3% processing fee:** "It's $X plus a 3% processing fee" — nobody refuses over it; at 10% margins that's +30% profit.
- **Two forms of payment:** offer to waive the 3% fee in exchange for a second payment method (fixes expired and maxed cards — the two big recurring-revenue leaks). Justification: "getting new payment info costs us man-hours; you save us time, we pass the savings." Get **ACH** as the second method — cheapest rail besides cash.
- **Gift-card delivery of the discount:** physical card, usable after ~3 payments, giftable to a friend (lead magnet); many forget to use it → full-price signup.
- **Lifetime discount at the churn point:** grant a permanent lower rate EARNED by staying past month X, where X = your average drop-off month; hype it as it approaches. Rice-company example: one-time price / 5% off subscription / 15% off after five consecutive months (set just past the common cancel point).

**Cancellation playbook:**
- Decide the policy ahead of time (notice periods, fees, cancel-anytime).
- Preferred policy: **cancellation fee = the discount they received for committing** ($600 in discounts → $600 to cancel anytime) — simple, equitable, resets them to month-to-month economics.
- Trade-off law: light terms → more sign-ups, more leavers; harsh terms → fewer of both.
- **Make canceling easy to find:** hidden cancellation → vanished customers and public 1-star reviews; a clear channel = a chance to save them.
- **Exit interviews:** offer to waive the cancellation fee for one; let them vent; get angrier about the problem than they are (they may talk themselves into staying); fix what's fixable; if the complaint is really "I wanted more," Rollover-Upsell into a HIGHER tier — upset customers do buy up. Saves about a third of would-be cancels.

### 3. Waived Fee

**Definition:** month-to-month requires a large setup fee; commit to a term (12 months minimum) and the fee is waived — but cancel inside the term and you owe it. Have customers initial the early-quit clause.

**Numbers:** fee = 3–5x the monthly rate. Worked example: $1,000/mo, $5,000 fee. Option A: $5,000 + $1,000 first month, cancel anytime. Option B: $0 fee, $1,000/mo for 12 months; $5,000 only if broken early.

**Why it works (risk swap):** month-to-month = your risk → the fee compensates; commitment = their risk → the waiver compensates. Joining today has instant value (fee avoided); past the midpoint, quitting costs about the same as staying — so they stay. Bottom line: customers stay when leaving costs more than staying.

**Rules:**
- Fee presentation: "It costs us money to get you started. If you only wanna test us out, you cover those costs. If you commit longer, I'll cover them."
- **Health check: >5% wanting to cancel early = product problem.** Pricing should nudge, not handcuff.
- Cash-flow dial: need more up-front cash → smaller fee (1.5–3x monthly) so more choose month-to-month and pay it; bigger fee → more commitments.
- Fee permanently drops once the commitment is fulfilled — earned free cancellation (keeps it equitable).
- Best for commitments ≥1 year and slow-payoff services (SEO, investing, weight loss) — keeps people committed "when they get emotional."
- Variant: early-cancel fee donated to a cause the customer hates (they name it at signup) — two reasons to stay.

## Building your Money Model

### The Three Stages
- **Stage I — Get Cash:** Attraction Offers get more customers for less.
- **Stage II — Get More Cash:** Upsell + Downsell Offers make more money from them, faster.
- **Stage III — Get The Most Cash:** Continuity Offers maximize total spend.

Why staged: Money Models grow with the business; launching a "finished" model from zero collapses on top of you. None of his businesses started past Stage I. Each stage must pay for the next.

### Five-step evolution sequence (don't skip steps)
1. Get customers reliably.
2. Make them pay for themselves reliably.
3. Make them pay for OTHER customers reliably.
4. Maximize each customer's long-term value.
5. Spend maximum advertising dollars to print money.

Warning: "when your Money Model starts working, your business starts breaking" — build operational reliability and the team in parallel.

### Four-step build procedure
1. **Start with an Attraction Offer.** Goal: turn strangers into customers and cover costs. Pick from the five; advertise it. Dialing in what works can take up to a year.
2. **Pick an Upsell Offer.** Goal: 30-day profits well above CAC + delivery cost. Solve the problem the attraction offer creates; offer at the moment of greatest need. Pick from the four.
3. **Pick a Downsell Offer.** Goal: convert the nos. Same price/change how they pay (payment plans, trials) or lower price/change what they get (feature downsells); alternate within one sale.
4. **Pick a Continuity Offer.** Goal: one last sale inside the 30-day window + recurring cash. Pick from the three. Fine if the right timing lands after day 30 — timing beats forcing.

### Assembly rules
- **Bootstrapped businesses MUST acquire customers at a profit** — without investors, a fortune, or free customers, a working Money Model is the only path to scale.
- **Perfect one offer at a time:** implement one, run it until reliable, then until automatic, THEN move stages. Measure in quarters, not weeks. "You either build it right or you build it again."
- **Raise price in stages:** launch cheap for early yeses + feedback → improve → keep raising price until the extra revenue from yeses stops covering the lost nos (i.e., until you make less money) — then step back one.
- **"Simple scales. Fancy fails."** 100 ways to offer one product beats 100 products (personal training: 1/2/3/4 sessions per week = many offers from one product).
- **Affiliate products fill gaps at any scale:** no product → sell someone else's; thin model → pad it; large business → add revenue with no ops. Examples: dental agency ↔ braces-manufacturer commissions; massage therapist reselling drop-shipped home tools; educator's software referral commissions.
- **Turn Attraction into Continuity via automatic renewal:** Buy-6-Get-6-Free rolls into month-to-month at month 12 — one offer, two jobs.
- **Mix and match freely:** upsell tactics inside attraction offers, downsell processes on every offer, continuity as attraction. Start with his sequence, then experiment.

### Reference Money Models (templates)
1. **Gym Launch (services/licensing) — the flagship, with revenue milestones:**
   - Stage I Decoy: free DIY courses/books/trainings, each with a free implementation call → on the call: free do-it-yourself vs $16,000 over 16 weeks done-with-you → $476,000/month within 3 months.
   - Stage II Classic Upsell: "Gym Lords" at $42,000/year, $6,000 prepay discount ($36,000 prepaid); community as Continuity Bonus.
   - Stage II Payment Plan Downsell ladder: prepaid → $10,000 down + rest spread → ~$800/week × 52 → start free via Continuity Discount (front-load free time until the first offer is paid off, then roll into continuity) → ~$1,500,000/month.
   - Stage III Menu Upsell + Feature Downsells: Full $800/wk; DFY advertising $300/wk; sales-team daily training $200/wk; monthly new releases $500/wk; original materials + tech support ("The Minimum") $100/wk — "almost everyone stayed for something" → $2,300,000/month within 14 months. Added Prestige Labs (own model, integrated) → $4,400,000/month by month 20.
2. **Micro gym (local service):** Win Your Money Back (pay-to-enter challenge) → Payment Plan Downsell (split-pay → three-pay → Trial With Penalty) → Menu Upsell (goal-personalized supplement bundle) → Feature Downsell (big bundle → small bundle → monthly subscription) → Rollover Upsell + Lifetime Discount ($50/mo off for life with a 12-month commitment).
3. **Newsletter (digital):** Free Trial ($0 then $399/mo after 30 days) → "Pay $297 now and keep that rate for life" — simultaneously free trial + pay-less-now + lifetime discount + attraction + upsell + continuity ("six-headed monster").
4. **Dog food (physical):** Buy 4 Months Get 2 Free → Classic Upsells (monthly toys? vitamins?) → Feature Downsell ("just the premium food then? you don't want anything else, do you?") → automatic month-to-month renewal after the 6 months, cancel anytime.

### Historical proof points (context, not benchmarks)
- Gym launch model: Free 6-Week Challenge ads at ~$5/lead to ~20 leads/day; ~50% of leads book and show; ~50% of shows buy a $600 program → 25% lead-to-customer; + ~$80 supplement profit → ~$680/customer within 48 hours (~$1 in ads → $34 out). Weeks later, offer the $600 back as credit toward an annual membership; 2/3 convert → full gym + $20,000/mo recurring from $3,000 down, inside 30 days; new location every 6 months, no debt.
- Storage-unit model: "free first month" yields $127/customer via lock ($47), boxes/tape/labels, moving affiliate + rentals, insurance upsell ($500 free → $100,000 coverage for +$10/mo, conditional on the lock), and one-size-up unit upsell. Lesson: monetize the free front end with pre-planned solutions to the problems the free thing creates.
- Rental car model: $19/day reservation → $100/day (5x) via vehicle upgrade, late return, premium insurance, minimum-insurance downsell, prepaid gas. Each offer solves a problem at the moment the customer realizes it.
- Attraction offers cited as turning $1,000 into $10,000,000 in ten months by re-investing returns.

## Hard numbers, thresholds, and decision rules (consolidated)

**Model-level:**
- Recoup full CAC in ≤30 days (credit-card float logic).
- $100M bar: one customer's 30-day profit funds getting and servicing 2+ new customers.
- 2x value × 2x customers × 2x speed = 8x; 3x each = 27x.
- Raise prices until revenue declines, then step back. Measure in quarters, not weeks.

**Attraction:**
- WYMB: ~10% will claim money back; only run if refund rate <5%; store credit ≈ cash-back in conversion; apply credit to a MORE expensive offer, spread over the term.
- Giveaway: discount = 10–30% of gross margins; run 3–7 days; claim within 7 days; use within hours–5 days; cap entries at 7-day personal-contact capacity; second (referrer) prize ≈ 2x leads.
- Decoy: free = more leads/lower shows; discount = fewer leads/higher shows; not making money fast → increase contrast.
- BXGY: raise prices permanently first; more free than paid; existing-customer bulk offers capped at 10% of base.
- PLN/PML: pay-now discount 20–50% + bonuses; >10% pay-later cancels = overpromised / conditions too easy / price too high.

**Upsell:**
- Burger: upsells took profit $0.25 → $3.00 (11.6x).
- Paid guarantee/warranty: +5–50% of price; example attach rate 30% at +10%.
- Menu (Economist): price the A+B bundle equal to the more expensive standalone.
- Anchor: premium at 5–10x main offer; bigger gasp → bigger purchase; no gasp → close the premium; main offer keeps primary features at ~1/5 price.
- Rollover: next offer ≥4x the credit (≤25% effective discount); gift card face value = 20% of the next offer's price; winback: $4,000 credit → ~20% take → ~$1.9M added annual revenue.

**Downsell:**
- Payment plans: 7-step ladder, up to nine total offers; temperature check 1–10 (8+ → payment terms; ≤7 → feature downsell); PIF count must hold when plans are added; re-run declined charges same day (recovers ~1/3).
- Churn by billing cadence (Profitwell, n=14,000): monthly 10.7% / quarterly 5% / annual 2% monthly churn → present most-cash-up-front first.
- Trial With Penalty: card + ID mandatory; explain fees after the card; per-miss fees ($50 × 10) over lump ($500) unless one miss ruins results; ~50% of unhappy trialists buy the higher tier on recovery; don't bill non-starters.
- Feature downsell: guarantee-removal case 25% → 75% close (35 full-price + 40 downsell per 100); remove features highest-value-first; downsold-to-fit customers = 2nd-highest LTV; never negotiate the same thing cheaper.

**Continuity:**
- Standalone at 1.33x/1.66x/2x/2.33x/2.66x the first month → 50/60/70/80/90% choose continuity; people pay ~33% more to avoid commitment.
- Bulk prepay: 1-in-8 uptake on "buy 5 get 1 free" = +50% 30-day profit.
- Bill every 4 weeks: 13 cycles vs 12 months = +8.3% revenue = +41% profit at 20% margins.
- +3% processing fee = +30% profit at 10% margins; waive it for a second payment method; prefer ACH.
- Lifetime discount earned just past the average churn month.
- Cancellation fee = total discount received; exit interviews (fee waived to attend) save ~1/3.
- Waived fee: 3–5x monthly rate (1.5–3x for more up-front cash); ≥12-month commitment; fee expires when the term is fulfilled; >5% early-cancel = product problem.
